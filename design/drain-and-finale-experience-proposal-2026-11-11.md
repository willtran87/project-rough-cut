# Under the Green / The Final Cut

Design proposal, November 11, 2026. Planning only; no new gameplay is implemented by this document. Durations and numeric targets below are starting hypotheses for playtesting, not measured results.

## Campaign decision

Keep three acts per playthrough, with two alternative second acts:

- Level 1 / Night Order -> shed -> The Back Nine -> clubhouse service garage -> The Final Cut.
- Level 1 / Night Order -> drain -> Under the Green -> irrigation keeper's room -> The Final Cut.

This requires two new playable chapters: the drain branch and the shared finale. It is more content than simply adding Level 3. Neither branch must be completed to unlock the other branch's ending. Chapter selection should make replay possible without repeating Level 1.

The current build continues shed victories into The Back Nine and leaves drain victories at the Level 1 result. Back Nine completion currently promises a future clubhouse continuation. Preserve both earned victories; new continuation actions follow the result rather than reclassifying an escape as a failure. The clubhouse becomes a short playable transition to its championship grounds, not a separate office level.

| Act | Main player question | Repeated decision | Emotional movement |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Level 1 | Where is Joe, and what did I leave behind? | Act, distract, conceal, relocate | Uncertainty to survival competence |
| Back Nine | How do I break his pursuit? | Commit to a route, evade, recover | Velocity, panic, temporary shelter |
| Under the Green | Can I cross the next water obstacle before Joe reaches me? | Read the discharge, judge his distance, cross or detour | Relief, dread, pressured timing, escape |
| The Final Cut | How do I use the course against him? | Read a committed pass, cross behind it, change the terrain | Awe, vulnerability, understanding, agency, release |

Protect this distinction. The finale is not a faster vehicle chase, and the drain branch is not Level 1 in a dark corridor.

## Design priorities

1. Every danger has an observable cause and an action that can answer it.
2. Dramatic changes alter routes, concealment or opportunity. Decorative effects support those changes.
3. Players can stop and understand a space from real shelter. Silence and relief give the next threat room to work.
4. Choices are local and legible: usually two visible options that reconnect at a landmark.
5. Familiar controls remain stable. Use, crouch, movement, listening and ball distractions carry the experience; no new quick-time input sequence.
6. Optional evidence adds context and aftermath variation; it never supplies the only escape solution.
7. Save before a new challenge. A failed climax must not require replaying its introduction or a successful preparation phase.

## Under the Green

The detailed [Under the Green encounter design](under-the-green-encounter-design-2026-11-11.md) expands the water crossings, slow hedge-trimmer pursuit, recovery, pacing and prototype criteria. Its specific chapter decisions supersede the summary here where they differ.

### Promise and setting

You have escaped the mower's reach and entered the systems that keep Joe's immaculate course alive. Joe follows on foot, slowly walking with a powered hedge trimmer. Timed water obstacles interrupt your faster movement and let him close the gap. The main objective is simple: follow the irrigation maintenance route to the clubhouse grounds.

This revision adopts the user's slow-walking hedge-trimmer pursuit and timed water obstacles. It supersedes the earlier surface-only Joe concept. Sound masking remains a secondary tactic; timing physical water crossings is now the chapter's central mechanic.

Build a shallow under-course network: root galleries, culverts beneath bunker lips, an open water-hazard trench and a pump room. Use recognizable golf objects throughout: lost balls trapped against screens, tee markers visible through grates, bunker sand spilling through joints, flags above inspection shafts, irrigation sector signs. Alternate compression with views of the surface. Avoid an uninterrupted pipe crawl or a sewer aesthetic.

Provisional first-clear target: 6-9 minutes excluding retries and optional reading. Adjust against actual Back Nine human playthroughs; equal narrative importance matters more than identical duration.

### Spatial structure

The grate refuge -> root gallery -> paired channel crossing -> pump house -> open irrigation trench -> keeper's room.

Each challenge is a compact loop with its destination visible or established before commitment. A timed wet crossing and a longer dry detour provide two ways through; both return to the same identifiable landmark. Looking back should show Joe's actual approach without requiring the player to abandon sight of the water warning. No visually identical maze junctions. The final destination is foreshadowed by a clubhouse crest and the underside of the 18th-hole scoreboard.

### Playable sequence

| Beat | Player action and choice | Threat / payoff | Recovery |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Grate refuge | Move, turn, inspect the protected Night Order; choose when to continue | The mower stops outside. Its engine shuts off; footsteps leave for a maintenance access. Escape remains a success. | Safe entry checkpoint; approximately 20-40 seconds of optional breathing room |
| Root gallery | Read pressure warning, let the discharge subside, cross to the dry landing | First water cycle teaches physical timing without a possible capture | A dry alcove overlooking the next crossing |
| Joe descends | See the hatch open and Joe descend a real stair; move while he starts the trimmer | The machine that could not fit has been replaced by a man who can. He starts walking, never sprinting. | Generous initial separation; reveal skipped on retry |
| Paired crossing | Take the short timed spillway or the longer dry retaining-wall route | Water creates a delay while Joe visibly closes distance; a good crossing earns a real lead | A loop permits retreat if the opening is missed |
| Pump house | Operate one bypass from a protected landing to lower the onward channel | Discharge redirects visibly; Joe must take a longer physical access around a closed maintenance barrier | Save after completion with a valid, reproducible lead; state remains completed on retry |
| Open trench | Cross two alternating water outlets using a broad central refuge, or take a longer perimeter route | Combine the learned timing with Joe's approach; no new water rule at the climax | The midpoint and detour prevent a one-shot timing trap |
| Keeper's room | Cross the final low-water opening and close the sluice from the dry landing | Joe reaches the far side with the trimmer, but the metal barrier physically holds | Safe chapter completion; machinery fades and the 18th's artificial applause becomes audible |

### Timed water obstacles

Prototype a roughly 12-second repeating cycle with a clearly warned discharge and about 6 seconds of safely crossable low flow. A typical crossing should take about 3 seconds at the taught movement speed. These are tuning hypotheses: test unfamiliar players, slower movement and every input method. Establish that pump noise means discharge danger here; it is not the old proposal's signal to enter the water.

- Start with a single transverse spillway: pipe knocks and rising pressure warn before water cuts across the path; a falling gauge and visibly receding water announce the crossing window.
- Develop the rule with a filling shallow channel, then two alternating outlets separated by a broad dry island. These are combinations of one learned cycle, not unrelated reflex minigames.
- Surface motion, gauges and optional captions communicate the same phase without relying on hearing, flashing or color alone. Keep the far landing visible through spray.
- A mistimed crossing causes one bounded shove to an authored reachable landing and a short movement penalty. It costs some of the player's lead; it does not instantly drown them or knock them into Joe's attack volume. Prevent repeated hit-stun within one discharge.
- Water remains shallow on playable paths. A surge is a force/timing obstacle, not a swimming or oxygen challenge. Off-route deep sumps are physically separated.
- Water affects Joe too. He waits at an active spillway or uses a visible connected service route. He never walks through the same dangerous jet as if it were decorative. One authored flanking route can create a surprise, provided the player sees the connection and hears him traverse it.
- Size the initial lead so one ordinary missed window permits a recovery or detour. A mandatory wait must not produce unavoidable capture after a previously successful crossing. Check this with the actual maximum wait and supported movement speeds, not only a staged arrival at the best phase.
- The dry route exchanges distance for timing certainty. It is safer when Joe is far behind; the wet shortcut can restore a lead when he is close. Both routes must remain plausible choices.
- Pump noise can mask relocation along dry shelves or behind walls, but does not erase a visible player. Once the pump stops, the trimmer becomes audible again from Joe's real position.
- Forward progress, good crossings and breaking sight genuinely earn breathing room. No hidden catch-up speed or resetting Joe immediately behind the player after each obstacle.

### Joe's presence

Use one authoritative Joe. He parks the existing mower, retrieves a petrol hedge trimmer and descends through a real maintenance access. The stationary mower remains outside. No simultaneous second Joe or moving occupied mower is allowed.

His ordinary pursuit is a steady deliberate walk, initially testable around 60-70% of the player's normal unobstructed movement speed. He does not break into a sprint or accelerate because the player is doing well. Water delays, corners and authored access routes create pressure. Budget the on-foot hunter, trimmer animation and underground navigation as required new work.

The trimmer has a recognizable long reciprocating tooth bar, held low with both hands; it is not a chainsaw or an extendable weapon. A restrained engine idle and blade rattle locate Joe. Brief contact with designated roots can shower clipped vegetation and delay his advance, making the golf-maintenance identity visible. Concrete, pipework and reinforced doors remain solid.

Sight and plausible sound provide evidence. Losing sight sends him to the last observation, followed by a finite search; it does not give him the hidden player's current location. A player who creates enough separation can stop at a protected position and understand the next obstacle.

### Loss condition and attack

Joe defeats the player when the active trimmer physically reaches them during a readable, committed close-range attack. Proximity alone, hearing a splash or being seen is not defeat.

He plants his feet, raises the bar and audibly revs before a short forward sweep. The player can retreat or step around real cover; the committed sweep cannot rotate to follow a successful dodge. A miss has a visible recovery before he resumes walking. Attack reach matches the visible blade, stops at solid cover and never extends through a wall or closed gate.

A hit produces a brief impact/capture presentation, cuts away and offers an immediate checkpoint retry. No mandatory gore, escape QTE or button mashing. Joe remains a slow walker even during attack setup; do not conceal a long-distance rushing lunge inside the animation.

Checkpoint restores must include water phase, bypass state and Joe's location consistently. Guarantee a valid starting opportunity and replay only the failed encounter, not the hatch reveal or a completed preparation action.

### Horror and agency

Use three authored beats: the mower engine shutting off, Joe descending with the hedge trimmer, and a pump stopping to expose how close its sound has become. The signature moment is waiting at a churning spillway, watching the pressure fall, and looking back to see Joe still walking toward you. Optional atmospheric details yield during these events. The player keeps control during every reveal.

One optional ball-screen alcove holds a maintenance record explaining why waterlogged ground is unsafe for heavy machinery. Picking it up is a small exposure decision with an obvious return path. The finale independently teaches the useful fact, so skipping this alcove never disadvantages progression.

No flashlight battery, rapid button mashing or random flood death. The primary loss is Joe's physical attack. A water mistake spends your lead and changes your next decision; a well-timed crossing makes you feel you earned the gap behind you.

## The Final Cut

### Core encounter

Joe is trying to finish mowing the championship hole with the player still on it. He commits to long strips and progressively removes soft cover. The player crosses behind those passes and floods a low service approach to immobilize his rig.

The finale's signature action is not running straight away from Joe. It is recognizing that a threatening pass has committed elsewhere, then stepping into the space behind it to gain ground.

Provisional first-clear target: 8-12 minutes including the introduction and ending. Build a much shorter playable slice first; remove repetition rather than stretching it to match this estimate.

### The machine

Working name: the tournament cutting rig. A deliberately exaggerated gang mower with a low central power unit, a small illuminated cab and articulated cutting wings. Start by testing a deployed width around 2.5-3 times the Back Nine mower's width, scaled to crossing times and reliable shelter clearance; art follows the successful dimensions.

| Property | Back Nine mower | Tournament cutting rig |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Threat | Close pursuit, interception and rams | Broad committed sweeps and removal of soft cover |
| Tracking | Follows current evidence through the road network | Selects the next feasible strip using authored mowing order or observed evidence |
| Steering | Pursuit turns and warned attacks | Slow headland turns; deployed wings cannot bend around a bunker |
| Escape opportunity | Missed attack and broken sight | Rear of a completed pass and its long turn |
| Terrain effect | Local cut marks and designated breakables | Authored cover sectors transition from standing rough to cut ground |
| Player advantage | Agility and narrower routes | Knowledge of the sweep, solid islands, drainage and lateral movement |

Avoid reproducing the damaged, spiked Back Nine silhouette at a larger scale. Tournament equipment should initially look unnervingly clean and institutional. Long reel banks, rollers, hydraulic linkages and safety lamps communicate how it works. Joe is small but visibly human in the cab.

### Arena layout

Use a legible dogleg championship hole with three permanent refuge islands: a stone bunker wall near entry, a concrete pump shelter on the inside corner, and a masonry starter's pavilion near the road. A peripheral service path connects them through longer, safer crossings. Shorter routes cut across rough and exposed fairway.

The grandstand, scoreboard, flag and public road orient the player. From entry they can see the destination and at least their next shelter. The arena is not an unrestricted circle around a boss.

A low nursery/service swale sits beside the final fairway. The player can flood it from the pump shelter. An elevated stone footpath skirts it and provides the escape during the lure. Establish the ground's weakness through visible rutting, seepage and a maintenance diagram, not a hidden trigger location.

### Playable sequence

1. **An empty championship welcomes you.** Both branches reach the same overlook through short distinct entrances. Warm lamps and recorded applause frame the course. The scoreboard shows the player's name and only facts the pilot's existing stations could have recorded. Never imply knowledge of private decisions or unseen movement. Give control and time to inspect the hole.
2. **The reveal teaches scale.** Two widely separated lamps turn out to belong to one machine. Its wings unfold. From protected cover, the player sees it cut a sacrificial band of rough and crush lightweight tournament furniture. The route behind it becomes visibly open. The first necessary crossing has generous timing.
3. **Cross a changing hole.** Two or three substantial crossing decisions combine soft cover, solid refuge and headland turns. A mower pass opens a new sightline or shortcut as well as removing concealment. At least one choice lets the player deliberately attract Joe onto a different feasible strip. Avoid a succession of identical hiding bays.
4. **Turn irrigation against the course.** Reach the pump shelter and open the bypass. The swale fills to a clear ready state, and sprinklers erupt over the grandstand. The player gets an immediate useful route change and a safe checkpoint. No scavenger hunt for three pump keys; this is one understandable preparation action.
5. **Choose when to bait the final pass.** From the stone path beyond the swale, expose yourself or use a supplied fixed groundskeeping bell. Joe must have a feasible approach and perceivable evidence before committing. The player sees his lamps align, hears the mechanism lock, then moves laterally behind the masonry shelter. The flooded state persists: there is no narrow expiration window.
6. **The player causes the catastrophe.** The rig reaches the saturated ground, loses traction and settles into the swale. A cutting wing tears into the exposed irrigation manifold, water lashes the machine, and its reels wind down under load. The outcome follows the player's setup and Joe's actual crossing. Once this success is registered, incidental debris cannot kill the player. Save the victory over the machine.
7. **A brief human coda from safety.** Joe visibly climbs out and reaches the already-secured boundary as the player gets to the road. Under the Green now supplies the campaign's sustained on-foot Joe encounter, so another chase here would repeat it. Prefer an observable arrival behind the locked fence, with no new attack or additional failure after the player's earned victory.
8. **Let the ending breathe.** At the road the player is safe. Joe is behind the boundary; no further attack follows. The ruined course is still being watered. The scoreboard reports `COURSE CONDITION: EXCELLENT`, followed by one last burst of applause. Evidence changes the subsequent account of the pilot, not whether survival counts.

### Sweep rules that make the encounter fair

- States: select strip -> announce/alignment -> deploy -> committed pass -> disengage -> headland turn. The trap adds bogged -> disabled. These states own movement, collision, animation and audio together.
- A strip selection uses the mowing schedule or a remembered observation. Hidden movement cannot silently retarget an active pass. Announced strips remain committed unless physically blocked; an aborted pass requires a fresh announcement before any new threat.
- Light position, wing posture, turf movement and mechanical sound show the actual danger footprint. Keep advance cues visible around key corners. Captions name the direction and phase where enabled.
- Warning lead time derives from distance to the next real refuge at the supported movement speed, plus reaction margin. It is not a universal two-second effect pasted onto every crossing.
- Turning is visible downtime. Crossings should fit inside it with margin; an apparent opportunity cannot be canceled by a surprise speed boost.
- Distinguish cover classes physically: rough conceals but gets cut; lightweight tournament fixtures can break; stone and reinforced concrete always hold. Demonstrate each before the player must rely on it.
- Cutting a sector updates concealment and visuals together. Avoid a grass image that still hides the player or invisible grass concealment after cutting.
- Permanent solid routes remain viable in the fully cut arena. Slow or cautious players do not reach an inevitable loss state after a hidden number of passes.
- Prefer two or three major terrain changes to constant destruction. After the authored soft sectors are cut, Joe repeats a playable route pattern. Progress does not depend on remaining grass.
- If the player baits too early, the rig completes an ordinary pass and turns; it does not consume the only chance to win. If they miss the exposure window, shelter permits another attempt.
- Both visual exposure and the fixed bell support the final lure. Golf-ball skill is an optional expression of mastery, never a precision requirement or finite-resource lock.

### Where the spectacle belongs

The destruction of the tournament belongs to the player's successful intervention: water spraying across empty seats, striped turf peeling under the wheels, the tournament PA continuing absurdly through the failure. A readable silhouette and one strong audio transition matter more than continuous screen shake or flying debris.

Use Joe's dialogue sparingly. One line before the opening cut and one during loss of control are stronger than uninterrupted jokes. During hazardous movement, warnings take priority over banter and the scoreboard. Joe's final appearance behind the boundary should have almost no speech.

Do not make every refuge fake, stack a second monster after Joe, or introduce unexplained supernatural geometry. The institution's absurd machinery and Joe's intent already support the escalation.

## Branch convergence and continuity

- Both entrances arrive at the same protected overview and the same essential explanation. Drain players recognize the water system; Back Nine players recognize commitment and recovery. Neither branch bypasses teaching the other skill.
- Give each entrance a short acknowledgment: water dripping from the drain access versus the garage shutter settling behind the player. No different health tax, locked ending or superior route reward.
- The Back Nine cart stays at the secured garage because the onward route is a pedestrian clubhouse/service access. Preserve the existing cart escape success and show the route physically; do not destroy the cart offscreen.
- Allow enough quiet traversal for Joe to relocate to the rig. Establish the equipment bay on the route and keep only one occupied Joe vehicle active. Do not imply simultaneous Joe positions.
- Persist campaign route, chapter completion, collected evidence and new chapter checkpoint. Do not carry pursuit timers or a nearly fatal transient state into a fresh chapter.
- Final checkpoints must snapshot pump state, cut sectors and challenge phase coherently. Restoring the player behind a now-missing hedge is not a valid checkpoint.
- Existing saves remain usable. A completed drain route should gain a continuation opportunity; old Back Nine completions can continue into the finale without being invalidated. Replay and restart must clearly distinguish chapter progress from accumulated evidence.

## Presentation and production scope

Use the existing fixed 3D geometry and pixel-surface art direction. Maintain truthful openings, visible mechanisms and shared collision footprints. Reuse controls, comfort settings, interaction feedback, evidence-led search principles and chapter result patterns where appropriate.

New work is substantial: a shallow above/below drain map with acoustic regions, an on-foot hedge-trimmer Joe with physical attacks, timed water obstacles affecting both actors, a finale map, an articulated rig, committed sweep behavior, sector-based cover changes, trap resolution and campaign handoffs. These are new behaviors, not assumed to be available merely because the current game has water and mowing effects.

Build water with authored heights, surface states and localized effects; use authored destruction states and pooled debris. Avoid fluid simulation, arbitrary terrain excavation, whole-course dynamic demolition or expensive reflective water as prerequisites for the experience. Load one chapter at a time and benchmark worst-case wet/destruction scenes on both hardware and software rendering paths.

Keep a single dominant danger cue. Pump audio, Joe's position and phase remain intelligible with music active. Reduced motion and zero flash retain the same information through stable lights, mechanisms and captions. Verify all routes and timing with touch, keyboard/mouse and controller; do not balance exclusively around expert mouse movement.

## Prototype order and decisions

### 1. Prove a finale crossing before commissioning the rig

Build one fairway strip, two solid refuge islands, one cuttable rough band and a primitive winged machine. Test announce, committed pass, cut state and slow turn. No cinematic intro is needed to judge it.

Continue only if unfamiliar players can explain where it will cut, identify a safe crossing and describe the experience as different from a vehicle chasing them. If the best strategy is endlessly sprinting away, redesign the layout and commitment behavior. If this still fails, remove the larger mower concept rather than adding more effects to it.

### 2. Prove a drain crossing

Build the dry/wet loop with a slow-walking placeholder Joe and one committed trimmer attack. Confirm players understand the discharge cycle, have a reason to choose either route, and can recover from one mistimed crossing. Test both actors waiting for water, the visible detour, attack collision against cover and the absence of unavoidable captures during mandatory waits. If waiting dominates, shorten the cycle or add actionable alternatives before extending the map.

### 3. Prove the final trap

Add the persistent flood state, fixed lure, feasible machine approach and lateral refuge. Test early activation, failed bait, a fully cut arena, zero golf balls and repeated retries. The trap must be legible without a walkthrough or perfect movement.

### 4. Connect complete chapters

Author only the rooms and crossing combinations that add a new decision. Add checkpoints, old-save continuations, both branch entrances and the ending. Test Joe's final appearance from safety for pacing; omit it if the rig's defeat and course aftermath already make a stronger ending.

### 5. Add and verify spectacle

Create final rig art, drain surfaces, tournament scenery and destruction states after geometry and timing work. Test high-resolution and compact layouts, motion settings, audio cues and worst-case performance in motion. Art must not introduce misleading openings or obscure established warnings.

## Acceptance evidence

| Question | Evidence to collect | Reason to revise |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Does the drain teach itself? | Unprompted player explanation after the protected first crossing | Players cannot distinguish dangerous discharge from the safe low-water window |
| Are both drain routes useful? | Observe choices and ask why; vary familiarity and input method | One route is consistently safer, faster and easier |
| Does a mistake teach rather than merely punish? | Observe missed water windows, close trimmer dodges and premature final baits | Immediate unexplained capture, water stun-lock, attacks through cover, stranded states or long repeated setup |
| Is the finale mechanically distinct? | Compare player descriptions with their Back Nine experience | Players describe only a larger/faster chase |
| Does the player own the victory? | Ask what caused the rig to become trapped | Players attribute success solely to a cutscene or an unexplained trigger |
| Can the slowest valid play still win? | Complete with all soft cover removed, no optional evidence and no balls | Required path lost, bait unavailable or damage unavoidable |
| Are warnings sufficient? | First-time attempts across input methods and comfort settings | Deaths before a readable opportunity to react |
| Does pacing breathe? | Observe waiting, safe pauses and repeated warnings | Constant maximum intensity or repeated compulsory idle periods |
| Is the coda worth keeping? | Compare endings with and without Joe's final appearance behind the secured fence | Players feel their victory was taken away or the finale overstays |
| Is continuity reliable? | Old/new saves, reload at every phase, both route arrivals and chapter replay | Repeated unlocks, lost evidence, contradictory Joe positions or invalid checkpoints |

Automated checks should cover state transitions, synchronized cut/concealment state, collision clearance, fully cut reachability, trap reset/retry, pause behavior and save migration. Human playtests must decide fear, clarity, pacing, route appeal and whether the surprises land. Neither screenshots nor deterministic bot wins establish those qualities.
